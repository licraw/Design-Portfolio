## What it is

Soft Arcade Synth Lab is a browser-based four-track groovebox designed to help people learn synthesis by making music. Drums, bass, pads and lead play together on a two-bar loop. A challenge rail introduces one concept at a time and asks you to change something and listen, rather than reading about it first.

Liam owned this end to end: product definition, interaction and visual design, the Figma design file, and the front-end implementation.

## The problem

Synthesizer interfaces assume you already know what an oscillator, a filter envelope or polyphony is. The usual alternative — a tutorial that explains synthesis before you touch anything — separates the concept from the sound it produces.

The design goal was to keep the real synthesis concepts intact while making cause and effect immediately legible. Five open questions came out of that, and each was explored before anything was committed.

## How should an unfamiliar parameter read?

Beginners think in perceptual words. Synthesis has real names. Hiding the real term makes the product useless as teaching; leading with it is the wall the product exists to remove.

Three directions were built and compared: perceptual only, technical only, and a permanent dual label.

<Figure
  src="/images/projects/synth-lab/synth-lab-exploration-parameter-labels.png"
  alt="Three parameter label options compared: Brightness only (rejected), Filter Cutoff only (rejected), and a selected dual label showing Brightness with FILTER CUTOFF · low-pass beneath it"
  caption="Exploration D. The dual label was selected: perceptual name leads, the technical name sits under it permanently, the value is always visible."
/>

Hover-to-reveal and a beginner/expert toggle were both rejected. Hover fails on touch and hides the exact word the product is trying to teach; a mode toggle asks the user to declare their own level before they have any basis for it.

The decision has a real cost. Every control is three lines instead of one, which is why the editor holds four parameters per row rather than eight.

## Sliders, not knobs

Synths conventionally use knobs. Convention on its own was not treated as a reason.

<Figure
  src="/images/projects/synth-lab/synth-lab-exploration-knobs-vs-sliders.png"
  alt="Rotary knob, horizontal slider and vertical fader compared as parameter controls, with the horizontal slider selected"
  caption="Exploration E. Horizontal sliders won on teachability: the whole range stays visible, so an explanation can point at where a value moved from and to."
/>

A knob shows only a current angle, and dragging one is a mouse-only gesture. Sliders are keyboard-operable, have an unambiguous drag axis, and reach a 44px touch target without growing. The accepted cost is that the instrument looks less like a synthesizer.

## One deliberate exception

Attack, decay, sustain and release are not four independent numbers — they describe a single shape over time. Splitting them into four unrelated rows hides the relationship that matters.

<Figure

  caption="The amp envelope is a direct-manipulation graph with the numeric values beneath it. Right: when an agent changes the envelope, the previous shape stays on screen as a dashed outline."

## Fitting four synths on one screen

Four tracks with their own synths is roughly fifty controls. Showing all of them at once has no narrow layout; showing one track at a time hides the fact that four are playing together, which is the point of a groovebox.

<Figure
  src="/images/projects/synth-lab/synth-lab-exploration-track-workspace.png"
  alt="Three track workspace options compared: all four track editors expanded, four compact lanes with one focused editor, and one track at a time"
  caption="Exploration B. Four always-visible lanes plus a single focused editor keeps the ensemble audible and visible while one track is being learned."
/>

The grid rows change meaning per track — drum sounds, scale degrees or chords — over the same sixteen columns, which are one bar of the two-bar loop. The opening challenge (above) builds a beat on the drum track before any synthesis vocabulary appears at all.

## Built on the design system

Synth Lab was designed on top of [Soft Arcade Design System V1](/work/soft-arcade-design-system) rather than inventing its own foundations. Type, color, spacing, radius and focus treatment all come from the system; Synth Lab adds only the components synthesis actually needs.

<Figure
  src="/images/projects/synth-lab/synth-lab-parameter-slider-states.png"
  alt="Parameter Slider component in six states: default, hover, focus, disabled, highlighted and agent changed"
  caption="Parameter Slider, the most-used control in the product. Two of its six states — highlighted and agent changed — carry the teaching load."
/>

Building the product also exposed four gaps that are broader than Synth Lab: a generic segmented control, a toggle, an anchored popover, and a reusable range control. Each is documented as a candidate for the shared system and deliberately left out of V1, so the baseline stays stable until a second product needs them.

## Agentic interface — an experimental POC

Synth Lab exposes semantic musical tools to agents through WebMCP instead of relying on DOM automation. An agent asks for a musical change; the product decides how to make it.

Agent changes go through the same command architecture as human changes, so they move the visible controls, produce an audible result, and participate in normal undo and history. Every change is explained next to the control it touched.

## What building it changed

The design file and the running product were not developed in one direction. Implementation pushed back:

- A one-bar loop was too repetitive to sit with while learning. The pattern became two bars, and the lane strip became a read-only summary of all 32 steps.
- The default tempo was too fast to hear a change land. It came down.
- Mobile audio exposed real browser and audio-lifecycle constraints, which is why the product has an explicit start gate rather than autoplaying.
- The implementation drifted from the designed control grouping and envelope interaction, and was brought back into alignment with the component system rather than the other way round.
